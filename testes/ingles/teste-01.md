## Texto

> Modern organizations increasingly rely on structured approaches to deliver complex undertakings on time, within budget, and to the satisfaction of those who will use their results. Project management, as a discipline, provides the methods, tools, and vocabulary that allow teams to plan, execute, and close such undertakings in a predictable manner. According to the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK), a widely referenced guide published by the Project Management Institute, every project moves through a life cycle composed of distinct phases: initiation, planning, execution, monitoring and controlling, and closing. Although these phases are often presented sequentially, in practice they overlap considerably, and monitoring activities run in parallel with execution from the very first deliverable onward.
>
> At the heart of traditional project management lies the so-called triple constraint, sometimes called the iron triangle, which links three variables that a project manager must balance continuously: scope, time, and cost. A change in one of these dimensions inevitably affects at least one of the other two. If a client requests additional features partway through development, for instance, the project manager must either extend the deadline, increase the budget, or reduce some other aspect of the original scope. Quality is frequently depicted at the center of this triangle, since it is influenced by the interplay of all three constraints rather than being an independent variable.
>
> Equally important to the discipline is the identification and management of stakeholders, any individual, group, or organization that may affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by a project's outcome. Stakeholders range from sponsors who authorize funding to end users who will eventually operate the delivered system, and their expectations are rarely identical. A project manager who fails to map these interests early risks encountering resistance, scope disputes, or outright project failure later on, even when the technical work has been executed flawlessly.
>
> Risk management also deserves attention, since uncertainty is inherent to any undertaking that has not been performed before in exactly the same way. Rather than eliminating risk entirely, experienced practitioners aim to identify potential threats and opportunities early, assess their probability and impact, and prepare response strategies ranging from avoidance to acceptance.
>
> Ultimately, project management is less about rigid adherence to templates and more about applying judgment to balance competing demands under conditions of limited time and resources. Organizations that invest in developing this capability tend to experience fewer cost overruns, more satisfied stakeholders, and a greater ability to learn from one project to improve the next.
