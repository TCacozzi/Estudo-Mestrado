## Texto

> Throughout the second half of the twentieth century, manufacturers around the world gradually shifted from inspecting finished products for defects to building quality into every stage of a process. This shift gave rise to what is now broadly known as Total Quality Management (TQM), a philosophy holding that quality is not the sole responsibility of a dedicated department but a shared commitment involving every employee, from the shop floor to senior management. Central to this philosophy is the conviction that preventing defects is far less costly than detecting and correcting them after production has been completed.
>
> Several individuals, often referred to as quality gurus, shaped the principles underlying modern quality management. W. Edwards Deming, an American statistician whose ideas found fertile ground in postwar Japan, championed the use of statistical methods to reduce variation in processes and popularized the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle, a simple yet powerful framework for continuous improvement. In the "Plan" stage, a team identifies a problem and proposes a change; in "Do," the change is implemented on a small scale; in "Check," results are measured against expectations; and in "Act," the team either standardizes the successful change across the organization or revises its approach and repeats the cycle. Joseph Juran, for his part, emphasized the human and managerial dimensions of quality, arguing that most quality problems originate in poorly designed systems rather than in careless workers.
>
> Decades later, Motorola engineers developed Six Sigma, a more rigorous, data-driven methodology aimed at reducing process variation to the point where defects occur only a few times per million opportunities. Six Sigma projects typically follow the DMAIC sequence, Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, and Control, and rely heavily on statistical tools to identify the root causes of variation rather than merely addressing symptoms. Unlike TQM, which is primarily cultural and philosophical, Six Sigma provides a structured, quantitative roadmap that many organizations combine with so-called Lean principles, which focus on eliminating waste and non-value-adding activities from a process.
>
> Critics sometimes argue that quality initiatives become bureaucratic exercises in which certificates and documentation substitute for genuine improvement. Proponents counter that, when properly implemented, these frameworks cultivate a culture in which employees at every level feel empowered to identify problems and propose solutions, rather than waiting passively for instructions from above. Regardless of which specific methodology a company adopts, the underlying premise remains constant: quality is best achieved not through a single sweeping initiative, but through countless small, continuous improvements sustained over time.
