## Texto

> For most of the industrial era, production systems have followed a linear logic commonly summarized as "take, make, dispose": raw materials are extracted, transformed into products, and eventually discarded as waste once their useful life ends. This model, while responsible for extraordinary gains in productivity and living standards, has also generated mounting pressure on natural resources and ecosystems, prompting researchers, policymakers, and manufacturers to explore an alternative paradigm known as the circular economy.
>
> Unlike the linear model, a circular economy seeks to keep materials, components, and products in use for as long as possible, extracting their maximum value before any of them are safely returned to the biosphere or reintroduced into another productive cycle. This is achieved through strategies that extend well beyond simple recycling. Designers are increasingly asked to consider durability, reparability, and disassembly at the earliest stages of product development, so that components can later be refurbished, remanufactured, or recovered without excessive cost or complexity. A product designed with circularity in mind, for instance, might use standardized fasteners instead of permanent adhesives, allowing a technician to replace a single faulty part rather than discarding the entire unit.
>
> Reverse logistics networks play a crucial role in this transition, since materials cannot re-enter a productive cycle unless they are first collected, sorted, and transported back to facilities capable of processing them. Building such networks is often more challenging than it appears, as it requires coordination among manufacturers, retailers, waste management companies, and consumers who must be incentivized to return used products rather than simply discard them. Some countries have addressed this challenge through extended producer responsibility (EPR) regulations, which hold manufacturers financially and operationally accountable for the end-of-life management of the products they place on the market.
>
> Critics of the circular economy concept caution against treating it as a panacea. Recycling processes themselves consume energy and sometimes degrade material quality, meaning that an indefinite number of cycles is rarely achievable in practice; aluminum can be recycled repeatedly with little quality loss, whereas certain plastics degrade more rapidly. Moreover, a truly circular system requires behavioral changes among consumers accustomed to low prices and rapid product replacement, changes that are not guaranteed by technology or regulation alone.
>
> Nonetheless, proponents argue that even a partial transition toward circularity yields meaningful benefits: reduced dependency on virgin raw materials, lower greenhouse gas emissions associated with extraction and processing, and new business opportunities centered on repair, refurbishment, and product-as-a-service models. As resource scarcity and climate concerns intensify, industrial engineers are increasingly expected to incorporate circularity principles directly into process and product design, rather than treating sustainability as an afterthought addressed only once a product reaches the end of its life.
