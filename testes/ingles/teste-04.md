## Texto

> Innovation has become a central concern for firms competing in markets where technological change can render established products, and even entire business models, obsolete within a few years. Scholars commonly distinguish between two broad categories of innovation based on the magnitude of change they introduce. Incremental innovation refers to small, continuous improvements made to existing products, processes, or services, refinements that extend a technology's useful life without fundamentally altering how it works. Radical innovation, by contrast, introduces a discontinuity: a new technology, material, or business model that displaces existing solutions and often creates entirely new markets in the process. The transition from traditional camera film to digital photography is frequently cited as an example of a radical innovation that rendered an established industry largely obsolete within a single generation.
>
> Managing these two types of innovation requires markedly different organizational approaches. Incremental innovation tends to thrive within existing organizational structures, drawing on established customer relationships, manufacturing capabilities, and distribution channels. Radical innovation, however, often struggles inside large, established firms, since it may cannibalize existing revenue streams, requires tolerance for a higher rate of failure, and frequently clashes with the short-term performance metrics that govern most business units. This tension, famously described by scholar Clayton Christensen as the "innovator's dilemma," helps explain why many breakthrough technologies have historically emerged from smaller, more agile companies rather than from the industry incumbents best positioned, in theory, to develop them.
>
> Investment in research and development (R&D) remains the primary mechanism through which firms attempt to generate both types of innovation, though the allocation of resources between them involves genuine trade-offs. A firm that invests exclusively in incremental improvements risks being blindsided by a disruptive competitor, while one that focuses solely on speculative, radical projects may struggle to generate the steady revenue needed to fund its own survival. Many organizations therefore adopt a portfolio approach, allocating the bulk of their R&D budget to incremental projects with predictable returns while reserving a smaller share for higher-risk, higher-reward exploratory work.
>
> Beyond internal R&D, firms increasingly pursue open innovation strategies, collaborating with universities, startups, suppliers, and even competitors to access knowledge and capabilities that would be costly or slow to develop internally. Patent licensing, joint ventures, corporate venture capital funds, and university partnerships all represent mechanisms through which firms attempt to broaden their innovation pipeline beyond what internal laboratories alone could produce. As technology cycles continue to shorten across most industries, the ability to manage this balance between internal development and external collaboration has become, for many firms, as strategically important as the innovations themselves.
