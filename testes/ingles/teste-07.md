## Texto

> Supply Chains Under Pressure
>
> A supply chain encompasses every activity required to transform raw materials into a finished product and deliver it to the final customer, including sourcing, manufacturing, warehousing, and transportation. For decades, companies pursued supply chain strategies centered on minimizing cost, often by sourcing components from a small number of distant suppliers who could offer the lowest unit price. This approach, sometimes labeled lean sourcing, reduced inventory levels and freed up working capital, but it also left firms with little slack to absorb unexpected disruptions, such as a single supplier's factory shutting down.
>
> One of the most persistent challenges in supply chain management is the so-called bullwhip effect, a phenomenon in which small fluctuations in consumer demand become progressively amplified as they move upstream through distributors, wholesalers, and manufacturers. A modest increase in retail sales, for instance, can lead a distributor to over-order from its supplier out of caution, prompting the supplier in turn to ramp up production far beyond what final demand actually requires. The result is a costly cycle of excess inventory followed by sudden shortages, driven less by real changes in consumer behavior than by distorted information flowing through the chain. Researchers have shown that sharing real-time sales data among supply chain partners can substantially dampen this effect.
>
> Inventory management sits at the heart of these dynamics. Holding too much stock ties up capital and increases the risk of obsolescence, particularly for products with short life cycles, while holding too little exposes a company to stockouts that frustrate customers and may push them toward competitors. Techniques such as safety stock calculations and economic order quantity models attempt to strike a balance between these two risks, though such models typically assume a degree of demand predictability that does not always hold during periods of volatility, such as pandemics or geopolitical conflicts.
>
> In recent years, firms have increasingly questioned whether cost minimization alone should guide sourcing decisions. The global disruptions of the early 2020s, which left factories without critical components and store shelves without basic goods, prompted many executives to reconsider the wisdom of relying heavily on single suppliers located far from the point of consumption. As a result, strategies such as diversifying the supplier base, nearshoring production closer to end markets, and maintaining strategic reserves of critical inputs have gained renewed attention. These measures typically raise costs in the short run, yet proponents argue that the resulting resilience justifies the investment, since the expense of a single prolonged disruption can far exceed the cumulative savings of years of lean sourcing.
