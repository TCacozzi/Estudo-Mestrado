## Texto

> Designing the Organization: Mintzberg's Framework
>
> Organizational design concerns how a company divides tasks among its members and then coordinates those tasks to achieve common objectives. Canadian management theorist Henry Mintzberg argued that every organization, regardless of its size or sector, can be understood as being composed of five basic parts: the strategic apex, where top executives set overall direction; the middle line, composed of managers who connect the apex to daily operations; the operating core, where employees perform the basic work of producing goods or delivering services; the technostructure, which designs systems, standards, and procedures; and the support staff, which provides indirect services such as legal counsel or cafeteria operations.
>
> According to Mintzberg, organizations can coordinate the work of these parts through several mechanisms, ranging from direct supervision, in which one person issues instructions to others, to the standardization of work processes, outputs, or skills. A fast-food restaurant, for example, relies heavily on standardized processes, since employees follow strict, predefined steps regardless of who is working a given shift. A hospital, by contrast, depends more on the standardization of skills, since physicians and nurses are trained extensively before joining the organization and are then trusted to exercise professional judgment with comparatively little direct supervision from above.
>
> Building on these building blocks, Mintzberg proposed a typology of organizational configurations. The simple structure, typical of small entrepreneurial firms, concentrates power in the strategic apex, with the founder frequently making most important decisions personally. The machine bureaucracy, common in mass-production manufacturing, relies heavily on a powerful technostructure that standardizes work processes to achieve efficiency and consistency, often at the cost of flexibility. The professional bureaucracy, exemplified by universities and hospitals, grants considerable autonomy to highly trained professionals in the operating core, since their expertise cannot easily be codified into rigid procedures. Finally, the adhocracy, frequently found in innovative firms such as advertising agencies or technology startups, relies on flexible, shifting teams of specialists who coordinate through mutual adjustment rather than fixed rules, allowing rapid adaptation to new projects.
>
> Mintzberg's framework has proven influential precisely because it resists the notion that a single organizational structure is optimal for every situation. Instead, it suggests that the appropriate configuration depends on contingent factors such as the age and size of the organization, the stability of its environment, and the complexity of the technical systems it employs. A young technology startup operating in a rapidly changing market, for instance, would likely struggle if forced into the rigid procedures of a machine bureaucracy, just as a nuclear power plant could not safely rely on the loose, improvisational coordination typical of an adhocracy. This contingency perspective, which gained prominence throughout the twentieth century, continues to shape how scholars and practitioners evaluate organizational design today.
