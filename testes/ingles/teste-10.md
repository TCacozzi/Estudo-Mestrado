## Texto

> The Changing Landscape of Electricity Markets
>
> Electricity markets differ from most other commodity markets in one crucial respect: electrical energy cannot be stored economically on a large scale using conventional technology, which means that supply and demand must be balanced almost instantaneously, every second of every day. This physical constraint has historically justified heavy government regulation of the sector, since a sudden imbalance between generation and consumption can cause voltage fluctuations or even widespread blackouts. In many countries, electricity was long supplied by vertically integrated state-owned utilities that controlled generation, transmission, and distribution as a single monopoly, justified by the enormous fixed costs of building power plants and transmission lines.
>
> Beginning in the 1980s and 1990s, however, several countries began restructuring their electricity sectors, separating generation from transmission and distribution and introducing competition among generating companies. Proponents of this liberalization argued that competitive wholesale markets would force generators to operate more efficiently and would ultimately lower prices for consumers. Transmission and distribution networks, by contrast, generally remained regulated monopolies, since it would be economically wasteful to build multiple parallel sets of power lines to the same homes and businesses, a situation economists describe as a natural monopoly.
>
> The transition toward renewable energy sources, particularly wind and solar power, has introduced new complexities into this already intricate system. Unlike conventional thermal plants, which can generally be dispatched on demand, wind and solar generation depends on weather conditions that vary throughout the day and across seasons, a characteristic known as intermittency. System operators must therefore maintain additional flexible capacity, such as natural gas plants or, increasingly, large-scale battery storage, capable of compensating for sudden drops in renewable output. Some analysts argue that intermittency, combined with falling costs for renewable technologies and batteries, is gradually transforming electricity markets from systems organized around a handful of large, predictable power plants into systems coordinating thousands of smaller, more variable generation sources.
>
> Infrastructure investment remains a persistent challenge throughout this transition. Transmission lines connecting remote wind farms or solar installations to population centers can take many years to plan, permit, and construct, often lagging behind the pace at which new generation capacity is built. Moreover, aging infrastructure in many developed countries was originally designed for a system dominated by a small number of large, centralized power plants, not for the more decentralized pattern emerging today, in which electricity may flow in multiple directions simultaneously, including from household rooftop panels back into the grid. Addressing this mismatch between evolving generation patterns and existing infrastructure is widely regarded as one of the central engineering and policy challenges of the ongoing energy transition.
